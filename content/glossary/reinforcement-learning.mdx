## In plain English

Reinforcement learning is learning by trial and error. A piece of software, called an agent, acts in an environment, sees what happens and receives a reward or a penalty. Over many attempts, it works out a strategy, called a policy, that earns the most reward over time.

Unlike supervised learning, nobody gives the agent the correct action for each situation. It has to discover good actions itself, and it often has to balance trying something new against repeating what has worked before. Rewards may also arrive late, so the agent must learn which earlier actions led to a good outcome.

## An example

Picture a research team at an Indian engineering institute building a simulated warehouse. A robot agent must move parcels from shelves to a loading bay. It earns a reward for each parcel delivered and a penalty for collisions or wasted time. At first it moves almost randomly. After many simulated runs, it learns efficient routes, which the team then tests carefully before trying them on a real robot.

## Why it matters

Reinforcement learning is used for game-playing systems, robotics and control problems, and it is one of the techniques used to refine large language models using human feedback. It gives students a different way of thinking about learning, centred on decisions and their consequences.

For organisations, it can be powerful but demanding. It usually needs a safe simulator or a great deal of trial data, and a poorly chosen reward can lead an agent to find shortcuts its designers did not intend.
