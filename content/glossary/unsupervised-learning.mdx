## In plain English

In unsupervised learning, the data comes without any answer key. Nobody has told the model what the right output is. Instead, the model searches the data for structure on its own, such as groups of similar items or unusual cases that stand out.

The most common technique is clustering, which puts similar data points together. The model does not know what the groups mean. A person has to look at the results afterwards and decide whether the groups are useful and what to call them. Other uses include spotting anomalies and simplifying data with many columns into fewer, more manageable ones.

## An example

An online grocery service in Kolkata has purchase records for many thousands of customers but no ready-made categories for them. An unsupervised clustering model groups customers whose buying habits look alike. When the team examines the groups, one might turn out to be households that order weekly staples, another occasional buyers of festival items. The team names the groups and uses them to plan stock, while handling the customer data in line with privacy law.

## Why it matters

Most of the data organisations hold is unlabelled, and labelling it is slow and costly. Unsupervised learning lets analysts explore such data, find patterns they did not know to look for and detect unusual activity, such as possible fraud.

Its results need careful human interpretation. A clustering method will always produce groups, even when those groups have no real meaning, so domain knowledge is needed to judge whether a pattern is genuine and useful.
