## In plain English

Most people meet AI as something that answers a question and stops. An AI agent goes further: given a goal, it can take a series of actions to reach it. It might search for information, use software tools, fill in forms or call other programs, checking the results along the way and adjusting what it does next.

Many current agents are built around large language models connected to tools. The OECD describes AI agents as systems that sense and act within their environment with some autonomy, use tools to reach goals and adapt to changing inputs. It uses "agentic AI" for the more complex case of several agents working together on open-ended tasks over longer periods. The idea of an agent also has an older meaning in AI and reinforcement learning, where it is the decision-maker that interacts with an environment.

## An example

A small exporter in Howrah uses an AI agent to prepare for a trade fair. Asked to "find three suitable hotels near the venue under a set budget and draft emails to them", the agent searches the web, compares options, writes the drafts and presents them for approval. The owner reviews and sends the emails personally, because the agent is set up so that it cannot send messages or make payments on its own.

## Why it matters

Agents can save time on multi-step tasks, which makes them attractive to businesses and public services. They also raise new questions: who is accountable when an agent makes a mistake, how much it should be allowed to do without a person's approval, and how to protect the data and accounts it can reach.

For students and professionals, agents are an active area of work in AI engineering. For policymakers, they are a growing focus of governance discussions, including at the OECD.
