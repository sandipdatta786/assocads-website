## In plain English

Most earlier AI systems sorted or scored things: is this email spam, is this transaction unusual. Generative AI makes things. Given a request, it produces a new paragraph, picture, piece of music or block of code.

It does this by learning the structure of very large collections of examples and then producing new output that follows similar patterns. The output is new, but it is shaped by what the system was trained on.

Generative AI can be very useful for drafting and summarising. It can also produce content that sounds right but is wrong, which is often called a hallucination, so its output needs checking.

## An example

A teacher at a government school in Howrah wants practice questions on fractions in Bengali for Class VI. She asks a generative AI tool to draft ten questions at three levels of difficulty. The tool produces them in seconds. She reads each one, corrects two that have wrong answers, and rewrites one that uses an unfamiliar example. The tool saved her time, but her checking made the material fit to use.

## Why it matters

Generative AI is changing how writing, design, software and customer service work is done, so students and professionals are likely to use it in their jobs. Businesses need to manage its risks, including errors, copyright questions and leaks of confidential data. Government bodies are dealing with harms such as deepfakes and misinformation. Knowing what generative AI does well, and where it fails, helps people use it responsibly.
