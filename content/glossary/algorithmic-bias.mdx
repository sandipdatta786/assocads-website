## In plain English

Algorithmic bias happens when an automated system treats some people or groups worse than others in a consistent way. It is rarely the result of anyone intending harm. More often it creeps in through the data the system learned from, the choices made while building it or the way people use its results.

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) describes three broad sources: systemic bias in society and institutions, computational and statistical bias such as unrepresentative samples, and human-cognitive bias in how people design and interpret systems. NIST notes that each of these can arise without any prejudice or discriminatory intent.

## An example

Suppose a company in Bengaluru trains a model to shortlist job applicants using records of whom it hired over the past ten years. If past hiring favoured graduates of a few colleges, or one gender, the model may learn to prefer the same profiles, even though no one programmed it to. Checking the model's shortlisting rates across groups before use would reveal the problem.

## Why it matters

Automated systems can repeat a biased decision quickly and at large scale, in areas such as hiring, lending, healthcare and public services. People affected may not know an algorithm was involved, which makes the harm harder to notice and challenge.

For organisations and government bodies, managing bias is part of using AI responsibly. It means examining training data, testing outcomes across groups, documenting decisions and keeping a route for people to question results. Removing bias from the numbers alone does not guarantee a system is fair.
