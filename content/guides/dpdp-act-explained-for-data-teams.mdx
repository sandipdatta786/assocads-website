India's first general law on personal data is the [Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf), enacted on 11 August 2023. It did little on its own until the government made the [Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf). For data engineers, analysts and machine learning teams, the law changes how data is collected, stored, shared, kept and deleted.

This guide explains the main duties in plain language, with the section of the Act or the rule number for each. It is general information, not legal advice. Every organisation's facts are different, so take advice from a qualified lawyer on your own situation.

## When does the DPDP Act come into force?

The Rules are dated 13 November 2025 and were published in the Gazette of India as G.S.R. 846(E). The [Press Information Bureau](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf) reported them as notified on 14 November 2025. Rule 1 sets three phases:

1. **On publication:** Rules 1, 2 and 17 to 21, which deal with the Data Protection Board of India, its members and how it works.
2. **One year after publication:** Rule 4, on the registration and duties of consent managers. That date has now passed.
3. **Eighteen months after publication:** Rules 3, 5 to 16, 22 and 23, which carry most of the duties that matter to data teams. This falls in May 2027. A proposal reported in 2026 to shorten this period for significant data fiduciaries had not been notified when we checked, so look for any amendment to Rule 1 before relying on the date.

The government brought sections of the Act itself into force on a matching timetable. The commencement notification on the [e-Gazette](https://egazette.gov.in/) lists the sections in each phase.

## Who does the DPDP Act apply to?

The Act covers digital personal data processed in India, whether it was collected digitally or collected on paper and digitised later (Section 3). It also covers processing outside India if it is connected with offering goods or services to people in India. It does not cover data used by an individual for personal or domestic purposes, or data that the person concerned has made public.

The key roles are defined in Section 2. A **data fiduciary** decides why and how personal data is processed. A **data processor** processes data on a fiduciary's behalf. A **data principal** is the person the data is about. **Personal data** is any data about an individual who can be identified by or in relation to that data.

## What are the main duties of a data fiduciary?

| Duty | Where it is |
| --- | --- |
| Process only for a lawful purpose, with consent or for a listed legitimate use | Sections 4 and 7 |
| Give a clear notice before asking for consent | Section 5, Rule 3 |
| Use processors only under a valid contract | Section 8(2) |
| Keep data complete, accurate and consistent if it is used for decisions | Section 8(3) |
| Take reasonable security safeguards | Section 8(5), Rule 6 |
| Report personal data breaches | Section 8(6), Rule 7 |
| Erase data when the purpose is served or consent is withdrawn | Section 8(7), Rule 8 |
| Publish a contact for questions about processing | Section 8(9), Rule 9 |
| Run an effective grievance mechanism | Section 8(10), Rule 14 |

Rule 6 lists minimum safeguards: encryption, masking or tokenisation, access controls, logs and monitoring to detect unauthorised access, backups, and security clauses in contracts with processors. It also requires logs and personal data to be kept for one year for detecting and investigating breaches, unless another law requires otherwise.

## How does consent work, and what is a consent manager?

Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given by a clear affirmative action, and limited to the data needed for the stated purpose (Section 6(1)). Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it (Section 6(4)). If consent is disputed, the data fiduciary must prove that a proper notice was given and consent obtained (Section 6(10)).

Under Rule 3, the notice must stand on its own, list the personal data item by item, state the specific purpose, and explain how to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Board.

A **consent manager** is a platform registered with the Board through which a person can give, manage, review or withdraw consent (Sections 2(g) and 6(7) to 6(9)). Under the First Schedule to the DPDP Rules, 2025, a consent manager must be a company incorporated in India with a net worth of at least ₹2 crore. It must not be able to read the data it helps share, and it must keep consent records for at least seven years.

## What rights do individuals have?

Data principals can ask for a summary of their data and of who it has been shared with (Section 11). They can ask for correction, completion, updating and erasure (Section 12). They can use a grievance mechanism and must use it before going to the Board (Section 13). They can also nominate someone to act for them in case of death or incapacity (Section 14). Under Rule 14, fiduciaries must publish how to make requests and respond to grievances within a period not exceeding 90 days.

## What are the rules on children's data?

A child is anyone who has not completed 18 years (Section 2(f)). A data fiduciary must get verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian before processing a child's personal data. It must not process data in a way likely to harm a child's well-being, and must not track, behaviourally monitor or target advertising at children (Section 9).

Rule 10 explains how to check that the person giving consent is an identifiable adult, for example by using identity details already held or a virtual token from an authorised entity such as a DigiLocker provider. The Fourth Schedule exempts some cases, such as healthcare providers, educational institutions for safety and educational monitoring, and real-time location tracking for a child's safety.

## What extra duties do significant data fiduciaries have?

The government can notify a data fiduciary, or a class of them, as a **significant data fiduciary**, based on factors such as the volume and sensitivity of data and risks to individuals or the State (Section 10). Check MeitY's notifications to see whether any have been named.

A significant data fiduciary must appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India and an independent data auditor. Under Rule 13, it must carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment and an audit every 12 months and send the main findings to the Board. It must also check that algorithmic software it uses is not likely to put individuals' rights at risk. This last point matters for teams building machine learning systems.

## How long can personal data be kept?

Data must be erased once the purpose is served, unless another law requires it to be kept (Section 8(7)). The Third Schedule sets a three-year period of inactivity for large e-commerce platforms and social media intermediaries with at least two crore registered users in India, and online gaming intermediaries with at least fifty lakh. Rule 8 requires 48 hours' notice to the person before such erasure, and separately requires data and processing logs to be kept for at least one year.

## What does the Data Protection Board do, and what are the penalties?

The Data Protection Board of India inquires into breaches and can impose penalties (Sections 18 to 28 and 33). PIB states that the Board will have four members and work as a digital office (Rule 20). Appeals go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal within 60 days (Section 29). Check the Board's official website for its current membership.

The Schedule to the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 sets maximum penalties:

| Breach | Maximum penalty |
| --- | --- |
| Failing to take reasonable security safeguards | ₹250 crore |
| Failing to notify the Board or individuals of a breach | ₹200 crore |
| Breaching the duties on children's data | ₹200 crore |
| Breaching the extra duties of a significant data fiduciary | ₹150 crore |
| Breaching any other provision of the Act or Rules | ₹50 crore |
| A data principal breaching her duties under Section 15 | ₹10,000 |

## What should a data team do now?

1. **Map your data.** List every system, dataset and pipeline that holds personal data, where it came from, its purpose and who it is shared with.
2. **Check the legal basis.** For each purpose, record whether you rely on consent or a legitimate use under Section 7.
3. **Review notices and consent flows** against Rule 3, including how easy it is to withdraw.
4. **Build in the rights.** Make sure you can find, export, correct and delete one person's data across systems, including analytics stores and training datasets.
5. **Harden security** in line with Rule 6, and keep access logs for at least a year.
6. **Write a breach plan** that can meet the 72-hour report to the Board.
7. **Set retention rules** and automate deletion where you can.
8. **Find any children's data** and decide how verifiable parental consent will work.
9. **Update processor contracts** to cover security and deletion.
10. **Take legal advice** on how the Act applies to your organisation.
