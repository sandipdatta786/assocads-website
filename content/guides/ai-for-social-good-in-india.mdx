Public interest in AI in India often focuses on chatbots and company announcements. Much of the practical work that affects ordinary people, though, sits inside government programmes for health, farming and schooling. This guide describes a selection of those programmes using only official sources, and explains what good practice looks like.

The figures quoted below are the ones published by the ministries concerned, with their dates. They are self-reported, so treat them as claims made by the programme owners rather than independent evaluations.

## Why does India focus on these three sectors?

In 2018 NITI Aayog published a discussion paper, the [National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2023-03/National-Strategy-for-Artificial-Intelligence.pdf). It chose five sectors expected to benefit most from AI in meeting social needs: healthcare, agriculture, education, smart cities and infrastructure, and smart mobility and transportation.

The paper argued that India's use of AI should aim to improve social outcomes, not only to grow revenue. It also noted that some sectors, such as agriculture, need public coordination because private effort alone may not be enough.

Since then, the sectors have stayed on the policy agenda. In October 2024 the Ministry of Education [announced three AI Centres of Excellence](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2064990), one each for healthcare, agriculture and sustainable cities. The healthcare centre is led by AIIMS and IIT Delhi, the agriculture centre by IIT Ropar, and the sustainable cities centre by IIT Kanpur. The government [approved an outlay of Rs 990 crore](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2064613) for the three centres over FY 2023-24 to FY 2027-28.

## How is AI being used in healthcare in India?

The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare has designated AIIMS Delhi, PGIMER Chandigarh and AIIMS Rishikesh as Centres of Excellence for Artificial Intelligence. Their purpose is to promote the development and use of AI-based solutions in health. The Ministry has described several tools in [a March 2025 release](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2113683) and [a December 2025 release](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2199422).

| Tool | What it does, as officially described |
| --- | --- |
| Clinical Decision Support System in eSanjeevani | Helps structure patient complaints and suggests possible diagnoses to the doctor during a telemedicine consultation |
| Cough Against TB | Screens people for pulmonary tuberculosis in community settings |
| Prediction of Adverse TB Outcomes | Flags patients at higher risk of poor outcomes when they start TB treatment |
| MadhuNetrAI | Analyses retinal images so that non-specialist health workers can screen for diabetic retinopathy |
| Media Disease Surveillance | Scans digital news for signs of infectious disease outbreaks and alerts district teams |

The Ministry has reported some results. In December 2025 it said Cough Against TB had shown an additional yield of 12 to 16 per cent in TB cases reported in the areas where it was deployed, compared with conventional screening. It also said MadhuNetrAI was in use at 38 facilities across 11 states.

In each case the tool supports a trained person rather than replacing them. A doctor still decides on the diagnosis in eSanjeevani, and patients flagged by a screening tool are referred for confirmation.

## How is AI being used in agriculture in India?

The Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare described its main AI initiatives in [a written reply to the Lok Sabha in July 2025](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2146922).

- **Kisan e-Mitra** is a voice-based chatbot that answers farmers' questions about the PM-KISAN income support scheme. The Ministry says it supports 11 regional languages and is being extended to other government programmes.
- **The National Pest Surveillance System** uses AI and machine learning to identify pest attacks from photographs taken by farmers and extension workers, so that action can be taken early. In July 2025 the Ministry said it covered 61 crops and more than 400 pests.
- **Satellite-based crop mapping** uses AI-based analysis of field photographs to help monitor which crops have been sown.

The agriculture Centre of Excellence at IIT Ropar is a separate, research-focused effort. Details of its specific projects should be checked on official pages before being cited.

## How is AI being used in education in India?

The Ministry of Education and its bodies use AI mainly to widen access to learning material. A [PIB explainer published in March 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/mar/doc202633809001.pdf) describes several uses.

- **DIKSHA**, the national school learning platform, uses AI for keyword search inside videos and offers a read-aloud feature for visually impaired students.
- **NCERT** has used AI and machine learning to translate Grade 1 and 2 textbooks into 22 Indian languages.
- **AI in the curriculum**: CBSE offers an AI skill module from Class VI and AI as an optional subject in Classes IX to XII.

Language is a large barrier to digital learning and services in India. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology runs the National Language Translation Mission, known as Bhashini. Its [Annual Report 2024-25](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/12/10fcadec462c330211502fed3d24ea83.pdf) says the mission started in March 2022 and aims to build a public platform for translation between Indian languages and English using AI and natural language processing.

## What are the risks of using AI for social good?

AI used in public services affects people who often have little choice about it and may not know it is being used. That makes the risks more serious, not less. The main ones are these.

1. **Privacy.** Health, land and school records are sensitive personal data. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to them.
2. **Bias.** A model trained mostly on data from one region, language or group may perform worse for others, for example on rural patients or on dialects it rarely heard.
3. **Errors that look confident.** A wrong pest identification or a missed diagnosis can cause real harm if people trust the tool too much.
4. **Unequal access.** Tools that need a smartphone, good connectivity or literacy can leave out the people they are meant to help.
5. **Weak evidence.** Results reported by a programme owner are not the same as an independent evaluation.

## What does good practice look like?

India already has official guidance. ICMR's 2023 [Ethical Guidelines for Application of Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research and Healthcare](https://www.icmr.gov.in/ethical-guidelines-for-application-of-artificial-intelligence-in-biomedical-research-and-healthcare) set out ten principles: autonomy, safety and risk minimisation, trustworthiness, data privacy, accountability and liability, optimisation of data quality, accessibility, equity and inclusiveness, collaboration, non-discrimination and fairness, and validity.

More broadly, NITI Aayog's 2021 paper on [principles for responsible AI](https://www.niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2021-02/Responsible-AI-22022021.pdf) lists seven principles, including safety and reliability, inclusivity and non-discrimination, privacy and security, transparency and accountability. In November 2025 MeitY [released the India AI Governance Guidelines](https://pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2186639), which set out seven guiding principles and recommendations for industry, developers and regulators.

In practice, good projects tend to share a few habits.

- They keep a trained person responsible for each decision.
- They test the tool on data from the people and places where it will be used, in their languages.
- They collect only the data they need, with consent where the law requires it.
- They publish how the tool works and what its known limits are.
- They measure results against a clear baseline and invite independent review.

For students and professionals, these projects show that AI for social good depends as much on field testing, data quality and trust as on the model itself.
