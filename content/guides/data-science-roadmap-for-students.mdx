A roadmap is a sequence, not a checklist to finish in a hurry. The order below matters more than the speed: each stage relies on the one before it. Adjust it to your course, your college's timetable and the time you have outside classes.

The stages assume a three- or four-year undergraduate degree. If you are on a different path, map the stages to your own years.

## What should you learn at school?

At school, the most useful preparation is mathematics. The [US Bureau of Labor Statistics](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/math/data-scientists.htm) names linear algebra, calculus, and probability and statistics as helpful school subjects for future data scientists. Its advice is written for the United States but applies equally to Indian syllabuses.

If your school offers computer science, take it. If not, you can begin programming on your own with free resources. Reading widely and writing clearly also matter: much of a data scientist's work is explaining results to other people.

## What should you do in the first year of college?

The first year is for foundations. Focus on three things.

1. **Mathematics and statistics.** Make sure you are comfortable with probability, descriptive statistics, basic linear algebra and calculus.
2. **Programming.** Learn Python properly: variables, loops, functions, and working with files and data.
3. **Habits.** Learn to use version control, keep notes and write short explanations of what you have done.

As an example of what a foundation stage can include, the [IIT Madras online BS in Data Science and Applications](https://study.iitm.ac.in/ds/academics.html) lists mathematics, statistics, computational thinking, English and Python programming at its foundation level.

## What should you do in the second year?

In the second year, start working with data rather than only learning syntax.

- **SQL and databases.** Learn to query, join and summarise tables.
- **Data handling.** Clean messy data, deal with missing values and make clear charts.
- **Statistics in practice.** Apply sampling, hypothesis testing and regression to real datasets.
- **A first project.** Choose a public dataset, ask one clear question and write up the answer, including what the data cannot tell you.

Free courses on [NPTEL](https://nptel.ac.in/) and [SWAYAM](https://swayam.gov.in/about) can fill gaps your syllabus does not cover. Under a 2016 UGC regulation, universities may accept credits from approved SWAYAM courses, so ask your department whether yours does.

## What should you do in the third year?

The third year is for core machine learning and practical experience.

- **Machine learning.** Learn supervised and unsupervised methods, how to split data for training and testing, how to measure performance and how to spot overfitting.
- **Deeper projects.** Build one or two projects that go from raw data to a result someone could act on.
- **Internships.** Apply for a summer internship. The [AICTE National Internship Portal](https://internship.aicte-india.org/) states that registration, applications and certificates are free for students. See our guide on [finding a data science or AI internship](/guides/how-to-find-a-data-science-or-ai-internship).
- **Open source.** Contributing to open source projects shows that you can work with other people's code. [Google Summer of Code](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/) is one global programme in which new contributors work on an open source project with mentors for 12 weeks or more; check its own pages for eligibility and dates.

## What should you do in the final year?

In the final year, choose a direction and prepare to apply.

- **Pick an area to go deeper.** Options include analytics, machine learning engineering, data engineering, natural language processing or computer vision. Choose based on what you enjoyed in your projects.
- **Polish your portfolio.** Keep three or four strong, well-documented projects. Remove or finish weak ones.
- **Prepare for interviews.** Expect questions on statistics, SQL, Python, machine learning basics and your own projects.
- **Consider further study.** A master's degree can help for research roles or a change of field. It is not the only route into industry.

## How does the whole roadmap fit together?

The table below summarises the stages. Treat the years as a guide, not a deadline.

| Stage | Focus | Output to aim for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| School | Mathematics, some programming | Confidence with algebra, probability and basic code |
| First year | Statistics, Python, good habits | Small scripts and notes in a repository |
| Second year | SQL, data cleaning, applied statistics | One written project on a public dataset |
| Third year | Machine learning, internship | Two deeper projects and internship experience |
| Final year | Specialisation, portfolio, interviews | A portfolio of three or four strong projects |

## What are common mistakes to avoid?

- **Collecting certificates without projects.** A certificate shows you completed a course. A project shows you can use what you learned.
- **Skipping statistics.** Libraries make it easy to train a model, but without statistics it is hard to know whether the result means anything.
- **Copying tutorial projects unchanged.** Interviewers recognise common tutorial datasets. Use a dataset or question of your own.
- **Ignoring communication.** A correct analysis that nobody understands has little effect.
- **Paying for courses before trying free options.** Commercial courses exist, and this guide does not recommend or rank them. Try the free public options first and decide what you actually need.

## Where can you get help along the way?

Your faculty, seniors and college clubs are often the quickest source of advice. Your college placement cell can tell you which employers recruit from your campus and what they look for.
